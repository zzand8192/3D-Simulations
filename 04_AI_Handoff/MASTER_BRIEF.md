## Greg's story function

Establish the event first. Then show Greg in a readable place with an ordinary goal. Let the event affect his task through the same assumptions established elsewhere in the episode. Give him a short recognition and response. Return to the broader simulation or reserve a connected object for a later payoff.

Vary his location between episodes, activity, entrance time, awareness, reaction, and whether he carries the final beat. One featured appearance, possibly with a background glimpse or later return, is a flexible planning default. No fixed appearance count has been approved. The old requirement for two to four appearances in different countries within every episode was based on an interpretation Isaac corrected.

His humor comes from timing and circumstances. A short look, practical question, or small adjustment can carry the scene. Make the scene readable to a first-time viewer and recognizable to a returning viewer.

## Attention mechanisms to use thoughtfully

1. One clear altered rule with defined time, place, scale, and exceptions.
2. A familiar object or activity that makes the rule immediate.
3. Visible progress toward a milestone, such as zero seconds, a target scale, or arrival.
4. Meaningful intermediate consequences that reward continued watching.
5. Numbers paired with physical comparisons.
6. A stable UI position with one dominant value.
7. New metrics when the story's immediate question changes.
8. Close objects and human tasks inside a larger event.
9. Greg's recurring appearance with a different situation.
10. Sound contrast at important events.
11. A specific final image connected to an earlier detail or to the premise.
12. A concise closing prompt after the payoff when appropriate.

Treat retention effects as creative reasoning. Do not promise views or claim these choices have proven causality.

## Story planning

Define the altered rule, onset, duration, affected area, and restoration behavior. Separate fictional assumptions from factual claims. List direct consequences, less obvious dependencies, and aftermath. Choose the ending early enough to plant its object or situation.

Plan each shot by the new information it adds and what visibly changes. A held camera is useful while the subject approaches, the environment changes, or a quantity progresses. Cut for a new scale, consequence, reaction, or piece of evidence.

For a typical 60–75-second plan, establish the question in the opening seconds, show a direct consequence, develop further consequences, place Greg after the rule is legible, and complete a connected payoff. Treat all timings as editable. Distinguish simulation time from screen time and label slow motion or time skips.

## UI and camera

Use a centered opening question, then a stable readout with small label, large value, units, and supporting context. Use a short caption to explain a consequence or add attitude. Preserve contrast over bright effects and avoid platform control areas. The exact source fonts are unidentified. Choose a production typeface and record it as a proposal until selected.

Give extreme-scale shots a familiar object. Establish a clear path from approaching threat to viewpoint. Revisit landmarks when comparison matters. Use first-person hands or props when they supply useful scale, activity, or response. Maintain scene geography and continuity after damage or restoration.

## Sound

Use the working narrator-free format. Plan ambience, action, short dialogue, event onset, approach, contact, and perspective changes. Choose one dominant sound for a major arrival and allow quiet moments room to register. Direct listening of reference tracks was unavailable, so do not claim an exact soundtrack match.

One measured reference, Download (24).mp4, increases audio energy about 17 dB between the second preceding sound arrival and the 0:18–0:19 interval. This illustrates timed contrast and is not a loudness specification for new production.

## Default deliverable for an episode-development task

Unless Isaac asks for a narrower artifact, produce the following in an organized sequence.

1. Premise and assumptions, including what starts, stops, and persists.
2. One-sentence audience promise and exact final payoff.
3. A timed story outline with cumulative duration.
4. Greg's scene with location, ordinary task, consequence, recognition, response, and possible return.
5. A shot table containing screen time, simulation time, camera, visible action, UI, dialogue or caption, sound, assets, and transition.
6. Factual checks and unresolved assumptions clearly marked.
7. Asset and effect requirements, reusing approved studio assets where possible.
8. A review of clarity, text readability, causal continuity, duration, Greg consistency, sound, and ending.
