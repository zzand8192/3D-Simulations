Ideas and performance

Develop an idea

Write the altered rule in a sentence. Define when it starts, where it applies, how long it lasts, and what still follows ordinary physics. List the assumptions needed to make the experiment coherent. Then choose the most useful examples to show on screen.

Score each criterion from 1 to 5 as an internal planning aid. Scores are judgments and do not predict view counts.

Criterion Question
Immediate comprehension Can someone understand the opening without background explanation?
Visual change Can three moments look substantially different?
Escalation Does the progression reveal new consequences?
Human detail Can an ordinary task make the effect personal?
Endpoint Is there a particular image or event worth waiting for?
Greg opportunity Can Greg demonstrate a consequence naturally?
Production fit Can the current assets and schedule deliver it clearly?

Select ideas that can be demonstrated. A premise with a dramatic title still needs a readable sequence of consequences.

Build a hook

Use a familiar subject plus a precise alteration. Examples of structures include 'What if [material] disappeared for [duration]?' and 'What if [ordinary object] became [multiplier] larger?' These are planning patterns, not a required title formula.

Sketch three opening frames. Test whether the question, main subject, and first change are understandable at phone size. Write what the viewer should expect to see later. Ensure the final sequence delivers that expectation or a clearly connected development.

Study real audience response

After publishing, record the platform, date, duration, impressions or reach when available, views, average watch time, completion rate when provided, shares, saves, comments, follows, and retention-curve observations. Keep platform definitions attached to the numbers. A replay can push average watch time beyond runtime on some services.

Derived watch-time ratio equals average watch time divided by runtime. It is an internal comparison and should be labeled separately from a platform-reported completion rate. Views alone do not explain whether the hook, audience distribution, topic, or ending drove performance.

Review the curve

If viewers leave early, inspect the opening's clarity and delivery of the promised subject. If a decline starts during explanation, inspect repeated information and readability. If attention rises near a return to Greg, compare the surrounding shots before attributing the change to him. If a closing card loses viewers after the payoff, consider shortening it in a future upload.

Run a controlled creative experiment

Change one major choice at a time where practical. Compare a visible opening consequence with a slower setup, or one clear UI measurement with several simultaneous figures. Record the premise, runtime, distribution context, and outcome. Treat small or mismatched samples as tentative.

The business should accumulate its own evidence. The reference pack provides hypotheses to test, and the upload log records what actually happens.

Studio brief

Purpose

Create cinematic 3D What If simulations that make an impossible change understandable, visually satisfying, and worth following to its final consequence. The audience should be able to identify the altered rule, observe its effects, and anticipate a meaningful endpoint.

Confirmed creative direction

Isaac requested cinematic Roblox-inspired 3D What If videos, a recurring supporting character named Greg, and comprehensive analysis of successful-looking reference examples. Greg wears a baseball cap, a blue hoodie, and black pants. He is often unlucky. After the simulation has developed, a cutaway can show him reading a newspaper, ordering food, or performing another ordinary task that the event interrupts.

Isaac clarified that Greg can be in a different place in each separate simulation, almost as though he keeps ending up lost. An earlier interpretation placed him in multiple countries during one episode. The later clarification supersedes that interpretation.

Isaac explicitly compared Greg's intended recurring function to the recurring tsunami payoff in the references. Viewers should learn to expect his presence while remaining curious about his location and circumstances.

Working production defaults

The retrieved prior chat contains assistant-proposed defaults of at least 60 seconds per simulation, no fixed maximum, no external narrator, natural dialogue, optional fictional news coverage, environmental sound, subtle music, and an elegant white serif opening question that transitions toward smaller informational labels. Preserve these as the starting production brief, with their status as inherited defaults. They were not all independently confirmed by Isaac in the available user messages.

The reviewed references support vertical composition and 30 fps as useful starting formats. Recommend 1080 x 1920 delivery at 30 fps for new production, subject to the actual platform and renderer. The source references themselves are 576 x 1024.

Audience promise

Each video gives the audience a clear hypothetical change, surprising consequences, a measurable progression, a physical sense of scale, and a particular final image. Greg adds recognition and situational humor across episodes.

Creative pillars

  1. Understandable cause and effect. Show what changes before asking the audience to track several consequences.
  2. Visible progression. Numbers and pictures should move toward a recognizable endpoint.
  3. Ordinary details. A meal, a newspaper, a phone, a cup, or a bus stop can make a worldwide event personal.
  4. Specific spectacle. Choose a visual that follows from the premise and changes as the scene progresses.
  5. Recognition with variation. Preserve Greg's identity and vary his place, task, entrance, and outcome.
  6. Consistent presentation. Reuse UI hierarchy, text treatment, sound logic, and production standards.

What the studio should learn from the references

Adopt the storytelling mechanisms and build original assets, scenes, and outcomes. The references are useful for studying timing, readable scale, UI, anticipation, and callbacks. Their downloaded watermarks identify the local footage but do not establish original authorship or ownership. Source attribution fields are provided for later completion.

Open decisions

  • Finished Greg face, body, cap shape, exact palette, voice, and rig.
  • Final channel display name, logo, platform mix, and release schedule.
  • Preferred renderer, animation workflow, and editor.
  • Whether each project keeps the inherited narrator-free default.
  • Final UI font family and project-specific export settings.

No revenue target, audience demographic, production team, upload commitment, or completed asset is assumed.

Camera, world, and people

Establish the world

Give each location a recognizable geometry. A straight road, open horizon, dam wall, park bridge, or pier provides an immediate spatial reference. Place two or three landmarks where the audience can compare the event before and after it occurs.

Use stylized Roblox-inspired forms with clear silhouettes, expressive faces, readable materials, and lighting that separates subjects. The reference footage mixes simple bodies with detailed environments and effects. Exact renderer, rig type, and asset sources are unverified.

Camera decisions

  • Start at a height that explains the subject and the viewer's position.
  • Use an eye-level view for personal consequences and larger views for scale or infrastructure.
  • Use foreground hands and props when they add a task, scale reference, or response.
  • Give movement a destination. A push toward the horizon or a turn toward Greg should reveal something.
  • Revisit a composition when comparison matters.
  • Preserve screen direction for an approaching threat.
  • Use shake, tilt, or blur when the event affects the camera's physical position.
  • Check clarity before adding effects that obscure the subject.

Plan scale

Write one known-size reference into every extreme-scale shot. A door, person, car, building, or landmark lets viewers understand a giant subject. A wide shot should retain enough surroundings to show why the size matters. If the subject grows beyond the frame, allow partial visibility to contribute to its scale.

Stage people

Assign background groups a purpose. Some continue their activity, others notice, and others respond. Use a small number of legible actions rather than undirected movement. Stagger reactions when people would receive information at different moments. Synchronize them when the premise specifically affects everyone together.

Preserve entrances, exits, gaze direction, and exposure to the event. A person behind shelter may respond differently from someone at the impact site. Characters should have ordinary tasks before the event interrupts them.

World continuity

Record what is broken, moved, flooded, consumed, or restored in each scene. Restoration of a material does not automatically move every object back to its starting position. If the fictional premise restores positions too, state that explicitly in the episode assumptions.

Practical asset reuse

Build a small library of locations, people, props, camera rigs, and UI elements. Reuse an asset when it fits the story, then vary lighting, composition, activity, and damage. Keep reference clips available for study and source assets traceable in the asset register.

Greg integration

The canonical Greg instructions live in 03_Greg. Keep his chosen place within an episode understandable. His different location across separate simulations can be unexplained. Introduce his scene with one environmental cue, a readable task, and his established silhouette.

Narration and dialogue

Use the inherited narrator-free production default unless Isaac revises it. Convey information through action, UI, natural dialogue, and occasional news coverage. Greg speaks briefly when a line adds something his gesture cannot. A news presenter can speak within the episode's world, with the broadcast clearly grounded in a screen or source.

Plan the mix with the story

Identify the sound the viewer is waiting for. Reduce competing sounds around an important quiet reaction or delayed arrival. Preserve audible speech. Change the sound perspective when the camera changes position. Check the export on headphones and a phone speaker. Edit for information

A shot remains useful while it reveals or changes something. Cut when the next shot supplies a new fact, scale, reaction, or consequence. Plan text hold time alongside action. Mark slow motion and time jumps. Use a brief pause before a consequence when the audience understands the impending result.

Ending and sound tail

Let the main sound resolve with the last meaningful image. Keep a short closing card only as long as its information needs. The source examples often retain sound through the card. Choose the tail deliberately and check that the file ends cleanly.

Story and pacing

Working default runtime is at least 60 seconds, inherited from the prior production brief. Extend when there are additional meaningful consequences. Reference runtimes vary and do not establish a single ideal duration.

Build the event chain

  1. State the altered rule and establish the ordinary setting.
  2. Show the first direct effect.
  3. Show consequences at increasing or contrasting scales.
  4. Cut to Greg after the audience understands the event.
  5. Return to broader consequences or a new dependency.
  6. Deliver an endpoint or aftermath connected to the premise.
  7. Add a brief closing identity or response prompt when useful.

This sequence is a planning structure. The placement, count, and duration of scenes should follow the idea.

Give each shot a job

For every shot, write what viewers learn, what changes visibly, and what question carries into the next shot. A held camera can work when distance, water level, crowd behavior, a readout, or a prop continues changing. An edit should reveal new evidence, a different scale, a reaction, or a consequence.

Example timing for a 65-second episode

Time Story function
0–4 s Premise and first useful image
4–12 s First direct consequence and measurement
12–25 s Two further consequences or contrasting examples
25–35 s Greg's ordinary task interrupted
35–48 s Broader development or second consequence
48–60 s Arrival, callback, restoration, or aftermath
60–65 s Brief closing card if the episode needs it

These timings are suggested and should change across episodes. Greg's first appearance should vary.

Handle altered time

Record simulation time and screen time separately. A five-second event shown over 25 seconds is 5x slow motion. Label the change so dialogue, physical movement, and readout progression agree. Record exact moment of onset and restoration. If the story jumps minutes or hours, provide a readable cue and update the environmental state.

Escalate with new information

Increasing camera shake alone repeats the same information. Change what the viewer can infer. Show a cup first, then a crowd, then infrastructure, or move from a large event back to a tiny damaged object. Each scale change should clarify a consequence.

Build the ending early

Choose the last meaningful frame before finalizing the middle. Plant any object it requires. A successful ending may be contact with the camera, an ordinary task made impossible, a changed environment after the rule resets, a surprising survival, or a small physical callback. Greg can carry the final beat when his scene has earned it.

Research and assumptions

Separate a fictional rule from the consequences inferred from it. Record quantities with units, sources, and assumptions when the script makes factual claims. The downloaded examples have not been fact-checked as scientific demonstrations. Their labels provide storytelling evidence for this handbook.

UI and typography

Purpose

The UI tells viewers what they are watching, how far the event has progressed, and what milestone they are waiting for. Keep the information hierarchy stable across episodes.

Suggested reusable system

Element Purpose Suggested treatment
Opening question Defines the episode Centered white serif, placed over the first scene
Metric label Names the current quantity Small uppercase text
Metric value Shows progress Largest element in the readout
Context line Explains units, scale, place, or comparison Smaller supporting text
Editorial caption Directs attention or adds a consequence Short sentence, optional serif italic
State word Announces onset, return, or arrival BACK, HERE, NONE, or an episode-specific state
Closing card Repeats identity and one response prompt Darkened image or dark background

The prior draft proposed shrinking the opening question into a smaller upper-left label. Treat that as an editable transition preference. Build the question and readout as separate text elements so each remains legible during the transition.

Starting measurements for a 1080 x 1920 canvas

Use these as layout trials. Platform overlays differ, so verify against the target service's current preview.

  • Keep essential text within a central usable region, initially testing roughly x 80–880 and y 180–1550.
  • Try an opening question around 64–82 px with two or three short lines.
  • Try metric values around 52–72 px, labels around 26–32 px, and context around 24–30 px.
  • Try captions around 38–48 px and adjust to remain readable at phone size.
  • Reserve the right edge and lower area for platform controls and descriptions.
  • Use local shadow or a restrained gradient where white text crosses sky, spray, or explosions.

These figures are recommendations for new production. They were not measured from the source films.

Typeface selection

Suggested pairings to test include Instrument Serif with Inter, or Cormorant Garamond with Source Sans 3. Select and record one pair once tested. The exact reference fonts remain unidentified. Confirm the actual font files and usage terms before recording a typeface as a studio asset.

Information design

One main number should dominate. Include units. Update the readout when the picture changes. If the story changes from population loss to approaching danger, change the label as well. Keep rounding stable. A precision level should match what the scene can support.

A joke readout can measure an ordinary inconvenience, such as a lost meal, but should remain brief. Avoid layering a joke counter over a moment that already requires viewers to parse several facts.

Caption writing

Use one idea per caption. Explain a consequence, name an unexpected dependency, or add attitude. Give the audience time to finish reading while the relevant object remains visible. Break lines at phrase boundaries. Keep subtitles for speech distinguishable from editorial captions.

Verify

Check the brightest, darkest, busiest, and most destructive frames. View the export on a phone. Check spelling, numerical continuity, font substitution, platform overlap, and whether a moving value distracts from the action it describes.

Greg's appearance and story guide

His function in the series

Greg is the recurring supporting character in Isaac's cinematic Roblox-inspired 3D What If simulations. Isaac compared the role he wants Greg to play with the recurring tsunami payoff in the supplied reference videos. The audience becomes familiar with a repeated feature of the series and starts anticipating it. Greg provides that recognition through a person, an ordinary task, and the episode's effect on his day.

The recurring audience question is where Greg will turn up in this simulation and what the event will do to him. A viewer meeting him for the first time should still understand the scene. A returning viewer gets the additional pleasure of recognizing him before the consequence arrives.

Greg should be recurring enough to become part of the series' identity. He can appear partway through, return for an aftermath, carry a final callback, or be spotted in the background before his featured scene. His entrance time and degree of involvement can vary with the episode.

Confirmed identity and design status

Isaac chose the name Greg, a baseball cap, a blue hoodie, black pants, an often-unlucky personality, and a recognizable supporting role. Claude will create Greg's final character model. Isaac provides creative direction and reviews the result. The Looks folder is intentionally empty.

Earlier assistants proposed a black forward-facing cap, a medium-blue hoodie with a front pocket, white sneakers, an age of 23, average blocky proportions, a simple expressive face, and a casual mildly sarcastic voice. These are working design suggestions. They are not finished assets or independently confirmed specifications. Keep the selected cap, hoodie, and pants as the confirmed visual anchors while Claude develops the final design using Isaac's creative direction.

Once a design is approved, record exact colors, proportions, face, accessories, rig, and voice. Reuse that model and its approved references. Lighting and camera angles may vary while the underlying identity remains consistent.

Different simulation, different place

Isaac clarified that Greg should appear in different places across separate simulations, almost as though he keeps ending up lost. One episode could place him in a city diner, another on a remote beach, and another in a mountain shop. The location is part of the recurring surprise.

An earlier assistant interpreted this as a requirement to show Greg in multiple countries within the same video. Isaac corrected that interpretation. The current guide changes his place between episodes. Within an episode, his location and movement should follow that episode's timeline.

There is no need to explain his travel between independent simulations. Each hypothetical story can begin from its own starting conditions. A recognizable landmark, room layout, weather cue, or simple location label can establish where he is now. The audience can enjoy the mystery without receiving a travel history.

Select places for the interaction they permit. A diner gives him a meal, a cashier, a window, and a bill. A bus stop gives him a deadline and a vehicle. A convenience store gives him shelves, a queue, and an object he wants to buy. A newspaper at a café provides a quiet task the event can interrupt. Choose a location that lets the premise cause a specific problem.

Establish the simulation before the cutaway

Let the audience see the altered rule work on someone or something before the featured Greg scene. Then the viewer can understand why his task goes wrong without a new explanation.

For a typical episode, his main scene can follow the first or second meaningful consequence. In one video that could happen earlier, while another needs more setup. Use a background glimpse before his featured scene when it improves continuity or gives attentive viewers a reward. Keep the first major information about the event clear enough that a hidden cameo does not compete with it.

The cutaway needs a connection to the main story. It can reveal an everyday effect, show the event reaching another place, contrast panic with a quiet task, or prepare a later callback. The scene should answer a useful question about the simulation as well as feature Greg.

The basic scene construction

Start with a readable task. Greg wants to order food, finish a newspaper article, catch a bus, carry a drink, pay for something, or wait comfortably. Show the object and his immediate goal before interrupting it.

Next, let the hypothetical rule affect the task. Identify the exact connection in the script. A missing material can remove a useful object. A movement rule can prevent a vehicle from stopping. An altered environment can change what a purchase or activity means. Keep the effect consistent with the assumptions already established for everyone else.

Then show recognition. Greg notices the problem, looks at the object or person involved, and makes a small adjustment or response. His response can be a brief line, an expression, a pause, or continued effort. The audience needs enough time to register what he wanted and why the event spoiled it.

End the beat on a change. The meal has passed him, the paper is wet, the bus has gone, or the ordinary task has become unexpectedly complicated. Return to the broader story, or hold the unresolved object for a later return.

His personality

Greg is an ordinary person who often experiences inconvenient timing. He has a reasonable goal and makes a reasonable attempt. His bad luck comes from the episode's circumstances.

He can be mildly frustrated, resigned, puzzled, patient, or briefly hopeful. A recognizable personality does not require one repeated expression. Let the severity of the event influence his reaction. He can recognize serious danger and act to protect himself. A scene can still be funny because the audience sees the relationship between his small goal and the enormous change around him.

Occasional success can make his next situation less predictable. He might get his food before the event, only to discover that the setting has made it inconvenient to eat. He might make the right choice and still receive the most awkward outcome. He can also adapt successfully when that contributes to the story.

Dialogue and silence

Give Greg words when they add a new thought. A short question to a cashier, a delayed recognition, or a practical request can support the scene. Keep the exchange short enough that the larger simulation can continue.

Silence is useful when the audience can already see what happened. An eye movement from the newspaper to a floating coffee cup, a glance at an empty plate, or a patient hand still reaching for an order can communicate the joke. Build these reactions as actual animation beats with a clear start, recognition, and response.

The inherited production brief uses no external narrator. Greg can speak naturally within a scene. He should not need to explain the whole simulation to the audience. UI, action, and other characters can carry the information around him.

Appearance frequency and returns

A return should change the situation. If his first scene establishes that he is waiting for food, a later scene can show what happened to the order. If the first scene establishes a newspaper headline, a later scene can show the physical consequences surrounding that headline. Keep the prop or goal recognizable enough that the audience remembers it.

Allow enough space between appearances for the main simulation to develop. Greg's recurrence works when his presence becomes familiar across the series. Repeating a scene without a new consequence reduces the amount of story the audience receives.

Production workflow

1. Develop the concept

Create an episode folder from EP000_TEMPLATE. Assign an ID and working title. Write the rule, assumptions, audience promise, and final image. Check three consequences and a useful Greg situation before committing to a full script.

2. Establish facts and simulation rules

Record sources for real-world quantities. Label deliberate departures from ordinary physics. Define onset, duration, scope, restoration, and damage persistence. Make a single assumptions sheet available to writing, animation, and editing.

3. Write the story and shot list

Use cumulative screen times. Record simulation time separately. Give each shot a new fact or change. Plan readable UI and captions. Select Greg's location and task. Choose what the final scene pays off.

4. Build a rough timed sequence

Use sketches, viewport captures, or simple blocked scenes. Include rough text and temporary sound. Check whether the story is understandable before detailed rendering. Ask whether the opening delivers the stated subject and whether the final image is worth the setup.

5. Prepare assets

List required locations, people, props, UI, rigs, effects, and audio. Check the studio register for reusable assets. Record model versions and sources. Greg's final model remains an open asset until Claude creates it.

6. Animate and render

Block major motion and camera positions before secondary acting. Maintain scale and screen direction. Add Greg's recognition and response beats. Render a short difficult section early to check text, lighting, effects, and render settings.

7. Edit and mix

Replace temporary footage and sound with selected assets. Time text to the action. Update readouts and time labels. Balance dialogue, environment, and major arrivals. Check the ending and closing tail.

8. Review and deliver

Complete the review checklist. Watch the actual export from start to finish on a phone. Record the filename, settings, duration, and remaining open choices. Keep a review copy and a delivery master inside the episode folder.

9. Publish and learn

Record publication information and actual metrics. Capture observations from the retention curve when available. Select one improvement to test in a comparable future upload. Keep the result linked to the episode.

The implemented look

The old project uses low-poly human proportions. Heads are rounded boxes with ears and necks. Faces are canvas-drawn decals with oval eyes, small highlights, eyebrows, and simple mouths. The standard torso is a tapered T-shirt with colored collar and sleeve trim. Arms are slim, hands are simple forms, and legs can wear long pants or shorts. Shoes can be selected or omitted.

The source comments describe approximately 5.4 model units as roughly 1.8 metres. This is a project scale convention. Preserve the actual geometry when reusing scenes rather than converting all coordinates from that approximate comment.

Character creation and appearance controls

The builder accepts skin color, hair color and style, top color and trim, short or long sleeves, long or short bottoms, shoes, scale, cap, glasses, coat, tie, backpack, swimsuit, headset, and selected text on the top. These are procedural geometry and material options.

The cap already includes a crown and projecting brim. Long sleeves already cover both upper arms and forearms. A long-sleeved blue top with black pants can be built using existing controls. The inspected builder contains no hoodie-specific hood, drawstrings, or front pocket. Those features require an extension or a separate approved character asset if Greg's final design needs them.

Acting and rig

The character has nested transform groups for hips, torso, neck/head, shoulders, elbows, hands, hip joints, knees, and feet. The pose() function sets their rotations and offsets. This is a procedural joint hierarchy built from meshes. No Blender character source or standalone FBX/GLB character model was found in the inspected snapshot.

Motion presets include idle, walk, tread water, swim, sit in a chair, recline, sit on the floor, cross-legged sitting, and lying on the back. mergePose combines a preset with shot-specific overrides. Props can attach to either hand group.

setFace() selects eyes, mouth, brows, and gaze, with automatic blinking and a mouth-shape cycle when a scene marks the character as talking. In the old project's presentation, speech is conveyed through bubbles even when the mouth animates.
